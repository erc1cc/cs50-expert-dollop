Strong vs. Weak Acids/Bases

Subtopic

Strong acids and bases dissociate completely in water, resulting in high concentrations of ions and strong conductivity. Examples include HCl and NaOH. Weak acids and bases only partially dissociate and exist in equilibrium with their conjugate forms; acetic acid and ammonia are common examples. Understanding the difference is crucial for equilibrium and pH calculations. Strong acids/bases are easier to handle mathematically, but weak ones are more common in biological and environmental systems. This concept is central to titrations, buffer chemistry, and identifying acid-base behavior in solution.

pH and pOH Calculations

Subtopic

pH is a logarithmic scale representing the concentration of hydrogen ions, defined as pH = -log[H⁺]. pOH similarly tracks hydroxide ion concentration. Because pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C), you can calculate one from the other. For strong acids and bases, you can directly calculate pH or pOH from concentration. For weak acids/bases, you often need to use ICE tables and Ka or Kb values. Mastering these calculations is essential for buffers, titrations, and real-life applications like blood pH regulation.

Ka and Kb

Subtopic

Ka is the acid dissociation constant, and Kb is the base dissociation constant. These values indicate the strength of a weak acid or base—the larger the value, the stronger the acid/base. They are used to calculate equilibrium concentrations of H⁺ or OH⁻ in solution, which leads to pH/pOH. The relationship Kw = Ka × Kb connects conjugate acid-base pairs. These constants are crucial for weak acid/base problems, buffer design, and titration curve analysis. Understanding how to work with Ka/Kb strengthens your grasp of equilibrium and solution chemistry.

Titrations

Subtopic

Titrations involve adding a known concentration of acid or base to a solution of unknown concentration to determine its molarity. At the equivalence point, moles of acid equal moles of base, and indicators (like phenolphthalein) signal this moment with a color change. Strong-strong titrations result in a neutral equivalence point (pH 7), while weak-strong titrations produce curves that must be analyzed carefully. Titration curves graph pH versus volume added and help visualize buffer regions and endpoints. Mastery of titrations requires both conceptual understanding and calculation skills. It’s a key lab skill and appears regularly in free-response questions.

Buffers

Subtopic

Buffers are solutions that resist changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. They consist of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base and conjugate acid). The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation (pH = pKa + log [A⁻]/[HA]) allows you to calculate pH based on buffer composition. Buffers are essential in biological systems, like blood, and in lab procedures where pH must remain stable. Buffer capacity depends on the concentrations of the buffer components. Understanding buffers integrates knowledge of equilibrium, weak acids/bases, and real-world chemistry.
