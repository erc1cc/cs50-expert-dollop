Endothermic and Exothermic Reactions

Subtopic

Endothermic reactions absorb energy from the surroundings, resulting in a temperature drop (ΔH is positive), while exothermic reactions release energy, causing a temperature rise (ΔH is negative). This energy change is due to bond-breaking and bond-forming processes. Observing temperature changes helps classify reactions and calculate heat transfer. These classifications also relate to reaction spontaneity and enthalpy diagrams. Energy flow is always from the surroundings to the system (endothermic) or from the system to the surroundings (exothermic). Understanding these distinctions is fundamental for calorimetry and predicting reaction behavior.

Enthalpy (ΔH)

Subtopic

Enthalpy is a measure of the total heat content of a system at constant pressure. Changes in enthalpy (ΔH) represent the heat absorbed or released in a chemical reaction. It can be determined experimentally through calorimetry or calculated using bond enthalpies and Hess's Law. A positive ΔH means the system gained heat; a negative value means it released heat. Enthalpy is state-dependent and does not indicate reaction speed or spontaneity on its own. It is a central concept in thermodynamics and reaction energy analysis.

Calorimetry

Subtopic

Calorimetry is the experimental technique for measuring heat flow in a chemical reaction or physical change. The basic equation used is q = mcΔT, where q is heat, m is mass, c is specific heat, and ΔT is temperature change. In coffee-cup calorimeters (constant pressure), enthalpy changes for aqueous reactions are measured. Bomb calorimeters are used for combustion reactions at constant volume. Calorimetry allows for direct determination of ΔH, which is critical for experimental design questions. Accurate measurements depend on insulation and accounting for heat losses.

Hess’s Law

Subtopic

Hess’s Law states that the total enthalpy change of a reaction is the sum of the enthalpy changes of the individual steps, regardless of the pathway taken. If a reaction can be expressed as the sum of two or more reactions, the ΔH of the overall reaction is the sum of the ΔH values of the component reactions. You may need to reverse or multiply reactions and adjust the ΔH accordingly. This law is useful when direct measurement is not possible. It’s often used in combination with formation enthalpies or combustion data.

Bond Enthalpies

Subtopic

Bond enthalpy is the energy required to break a bond in a mole of gaseous molecules. Calculating the overall enthalpy change of a reaction involves subtracting the total bond energies of products from reactants. Bond breaking requires energy (endothermic), while bond formation releases energy (exothermic). Using average bond enthalpies provides an estimate of ΔH, useful when experimental data isn’t available. This approach gives insight into the stability and reactivity of molecules. It connects thermodynamics to molecular structure.
