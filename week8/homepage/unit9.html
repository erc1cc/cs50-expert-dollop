Spontaneity and ΔG

Subtopic

Gibbs Free Energy (ΔG) determines whether a reaction is thermodynamically spontaneous under constant temperature and pressure. A negative ΔG means a reaction is spontaneous, while a positive value means it is non-spontaneous. ΔG depends on both enthalpy (ΔH) and entropy (ΔS): ΔG = ΔH - TΔS. Reactions may be enthalpy-driven, entropy-driven, or both. Temperature can influence spontaneity, especially when ΔH and ΔS have opposing signs. Understanding ΔG helps connect equilibrium, electrochemistry, and thermodynamics into one framework.

Entropy (ΔS)

Subtopic

Entropy is a measure of the disorder or randomness of a system. Processes that increase disorder (like melting, vaporization, or mixing) have positive ΔS. Reactions where the number of gas particles increases or where energy is dispersed often show increased entropy. ΔS is part of the Gibbs Free Energy equation and is key in predicting whether a reaction becomes more or less favorable with temperature. Though less intuitive than enthalpy, entropy is fundamental to understanding why some processes are irreversible. Mastery of entropy allows for deeper insight into reaction direction and spontaneity.

Free Energy and Equilibrium

Subtopic

There’s a direct relationship between Gibbs Free Energy and the equilibrium constant: ΔG = -RT ln K. A large K corresponds to a negative ΔG (spontaneous), while a small K corresponds to a positive ΔG (non-spontaneous). At equilibrium, ΔG = 0, meaning no net change in free energy. This relationship unifies equilibrium and thermodynamics, showing that reactions naturally move toward states of lower free energy. Calculating ΔG from K (or vice versa) is common in AP questions. Understanding this topic is essential for tying together multiple units.

Electrochemical Cells

Subtopic

Electrochemical cells use redox reactions to either generate or consume electrical energy. Voltaic (galvanic) cells are spontaneous and produce electricity, while electrolytic cells require external energy to drive non-spontaneous reactions. These cells involve two half-reactions, separated into anode (oxidation) and cathode (reduction), connected by a salt bridge. Electrons flow from anode to cathode, and ion movement maintains electrical neutrality. Diagramming and analyzing cells reinforces understanding of redox, spontaneity, and energy transformation. These concepts are highly testable and lab-relevant.

Standard Cell Potentials (E°cell)

Subtopic

The standard cell potential is the voltage produced by a voltaic cell under standard conditions. It’s calculated by subtracting the standard reduction potential of the anode from the cathode: E°cell = E°cathode - E°anode. Positive E°cell values indicate spontaneous reactions. You can relate E°cell to ΔG using the equation ΔG = -nFE°cell, where n is moles of electrons and F is Faraday’s constant. Standard potentials are tabulated and used to rank oxidizing and reducing agents. This topic merges electrochemistry with thermodynamics.

Faraday’s Law

Subtopic

Faraday’s Law relates the amount of chemical change in electrolysis to the amount of electric charge passed. It’s based on the idea that one mole of electrons carries 96,485 coulombs (1 Faraday). Using current and time, you can calculate the moles of electrons transferred, and from that, the amount of substance oxidized or reduced. This is essential for quantitative electrochemistry problems. Applications include metal plating, electrolysis of water, and industrial extraction of metals. Mastery of Faraday’s Law combines stoichiometry, redox, and electrical concepts.
