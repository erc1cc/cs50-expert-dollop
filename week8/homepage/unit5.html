Reaction Rates

Subtopic

Reaction rate measures how quickly reactants are converted into products, typically expressed as change in concentration over time. Rates can be affected by temperature, concentration, surface area, catalysts, and the nature of the reactants. Initial rates are often used to determine how the concentration of reactants affects the speed of the reaction. Monitoring reaction rates helps chemists control industrial processes and understand mechanisms. Rates are experimentally determined and used to develop rate laws. Understanding this topic requires both graph interpretation and algebraic manipulation.

Rate Laws

Subtopic

Rate laws express how the concentration of reactants affects the rate of a reaction. A general form is rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, where k is the rate constant and m and n are reaction orders, determined experimentally. These orders do not necessarily match the coefficients in the balanced chemical equation. Zero-, first-, and second-order reactions behave differently and are graphed in different ways. The overall order (sum of exponents) gives insight into the complexity of the reaction. Being able to determine rate laws from data is a key skill in AP Chem.

Integrated Rate Laws

Subtopic

Integrated rate laws allow you to calculate the concentration of reactants at any point in time. Each reaction order (zero, first, second) has a unique equation and graphical representation. For example, first-order reactions show a linear plot of ln[A] vs. time. These laws are also used to calculate half-life, the time it takes for half the reactant to disappear. Understanding integrated rate laws allows for deeper insight into reaction dynamics and decay processes. They often appear on the exam in data-based problems.

Collision Theory

Subtopic

This theory states that for a reaction to occur, particles must collide with sufficient energy and proper orientation. The minimum energy required for a successful collision is called the activation energy. Increasing temperature raises particle speed and collision frequency, thus increasing the rate. Effective collisions are relatively rare, so anything that improves their frequency or effectiveness (like concentration or catalysts) speeds up the reaction. Collision theory explains why some reactions occur quickly while others are very slow. It connects theoretical understanding with observable reaction behavior.

Activation Energy and Energy Diagrams

Subtopic

Energy diagrams show the progress of a reaction along a reaction coordinate, highlighting the energy barrier (activation energy) and the difference between reactants and products. Endothermic reactions absorb energy (products higher than reactants), while exothermic ones release energy. The activation energy is the "hill" reactants must climb before transforming into products. Catalysts lower this hill, increasing reaction rate without being consumed. Energy diagrams help visualize energy changes and reaction feasibility. They're also helpful for understanding mechanisms and multi-step reactions.

Reaction Mechanisms

Subtopic

Mechanisms break a complex reaction into a sequence of elementary steps, each with its own molecularity and rate law. The slowest step is the rate-determining step, and its rate law matches the observed overall rate law. Intermediates are species produced in one step and consumed in another—they do not appear in the net equation. Studying mechanisms helps explain why some reactions are faster than others and how catalysts work. This topic connects to rate laws and activation energy, bringing together several parts of kinetics. Predicting or proposing mechanisms is a common skill assessed in free-response questions.
